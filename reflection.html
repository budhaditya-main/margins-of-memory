Margins Of Memory Return to archive

Project Reflection

Reflection

The making of Margins of Memory: A Digital Archive of Indian Political Cartoons transformed the students' understanding of the archive from a space that simply preserves material into a  system that actively shapes how material is remembered. At the beginning of the project, the counter-archive was primarily understood through the question of what remained outside the existing collection. However, the practical process of constructing the archive revealed that creating an alternative archive does not simply mean adding what is absent or deemed unequipped. But rather, it also involved decisions about what should be collected, how it should be described, which categories should be used, what sources can be trusted and how the material should be accessed. The project therefore made the theoretical question of archival power more immediate because the student was no longer only studying the archive but participating in the construction of one.

This became particularly clear through Stoler’s discussion between treating the archive as a source and treating the archive as a subject. They necessarily argued that archival research must move beyond treating archives merely as repositories of information and instead examine the processes through which knowledge in itself is organized and authorized ( Stoler, 93). The idea initially informed the project’s examination of the Indian Cartoon Gallery (ICG), where competitive selection benchmark contributes to what cartoons become visible within the institutional walls of the exhibition. However, while  constructing Margins Of Memory, the same question became applicable to the counter-archive itself. The digital archive, as mentioned, thus also transformed into creating a system of visibility. Decisions to record region. Language, platform, date, selection history within the competitive sphere and lastly provenance; determined which aspects of a cartoon would become part of its historical record. The archive could therefore not exist completely outside the structures of power that it sought to question largely.

Further to this notion, the argument that Mbembe perceives that the archive emerges through process of selection, judgement and authority demonstrates that preservation is never entirely separate from decisions about what is considered worthy of being retained( Mbembe, 20). This became particularly relevant when the project began cataloguing individual cartoons. The student then came in the position to determine which contributed works fell within the scope of the collection and what information was necessary for each record. Even the decision to preserve the ICG selection history became a curatorial choice. At the same time, the project required caution against reproducing the same hierarchy that it sought to examine. A cartoon that had not appeared in the gallery could not automatically be described as “ rejected.” since such a description would require evidence that was not always available. Recording statuses such as “submitted but not selected” instead allowed the relationship between the digital archive and the existing institution to remain visible without turning competitive recognition into the measure of its historical value.

The participatory dimension of the project also altered the understanding of authorship and archival description. Drawing in from R. Srinivasan’s discussion of multiple voices; especially considering his ethnographic work with the zulu community; emphasizing on the importance of allowing communities to participate in the production and organization of knowledge rather than treating them simply as passive users of technological systems ( Srinivasan, 163-65). This became relevant to the decision to invite cartoonists to contribute both their works and the provenance attached to it. A political cartoon can contain references to a political event, expression, readership or controversy that may not remain intelligible through just the image alone.  The inclusion of contributor provided context therefore became more than just a method of collecting additional information; it became an attempt to avoid allowing the archive’s institutional categories to become the only framework through which the cartoons can be understood. 

Now, the most significant ethical consideration emerged around access and ownership because Margins of Memory is a digital archive, accessibility could initially appear to be an uncomplicated archival objective. Kimberly Christen's discussion of Mukurtu however challenges the assumption that digitisation should automatically result in unrestricted access. Her argument emphasises on the importance of communities having control over how cultural material are circulated and accessed ( Christen, 2887). Although Christen’s discussion is towards indigenous systems and cannot be directly equated with political cartoons, the principle became useful for reconsidering the difference between displaying the material and having the authority to circulate it. This has become relevant for cartoons originating from instagram, newspaper, exhibitions and other digital platforms. Consequently, provenance and access cannot be treated as identical questions. Knowing where an image originated does not automatically establish the right to reproduce it. The project therefore identified rights, permissions and access conditions as areas; requiring further development. 

Now, consequently drawing from Neils Brugger's notion of the greater awareness of instability of digital material. He necessarily talks about the web as a historical source ; that becomes particularly relevant to an archive containing cartoons that originally circulated through digital platforms. Brugger argues that the web itself can become an important source for future historical research, while also requiring attention to the problems involved in preserving and interpreting material from an unstable digital environment (Bruggers, 102). This perspective made the digital circulation of political cartoons  appear less permanent than it initially seemed. Social Media posts can disappear, links can become inaccessible, platforms can change and an image can become separated from the political moment in which it was originally circulated. In addition, metadata, dates, source links, provenance became important components of preservation rather than just supplementary information.

The process holds several gaps within the project itself. Margins of Memory cannot fully represent the full diversity of Indian political cartooning, particularly within a country characterised by multiple linguistic, regional and political contexts; that in turn can involve national events as well. The collection is entirely dependent on available contributions and its interface inevitably influences how users encounter the material. The student therefore identified several areas for future development: Clearer rights and access categories, a more systematic preservation strategy, stronger metadata structures and a broader participation from cartoonists across linguistic and national contexts. Greater attention, thus could also be given to documenting uncertainty rather than attempting to resolve every archival gap. Such uncertainty is itself part of the history of how an object enters an archive. 

Ultimately, in conclusion the project demonstrates that a digital archive; cannot become neutral simply by attempting to be more inclusive. The process of archiving remains selective even when it is designed to question an existing system of selection. Margins of Memory consequently transformed archival theory into an abstract discussion of power into a practical problem of description, classification, provenance, participation and access. The most significant learning emerged from recognising the archival power cannot simply be removed from the process; instead; its conditions can be made more visible and open to questioning. The project therefore became not only an attempt to preserve political cartoons outside of competitive institutional framework, but also an exercise in understanding how every act of preservation creates another structure through which the  past can be encountered.
