About the archive

Rationale

Margins Of Memory: A Digital Archive Of Indian Political Cartoons develops from the earlier counter-archive project concerned with the relationship between archival selection and cultural visibility. The project, during its initial creation, took Indian Cartoon Gallery (ICG), Bengaluru, as an important point of departure, not relatively suggesting that the institution deliberately excludes particular cartoonists, but to consider how exhibition and competition structures necessarily determine which works become visible within the said institutional framework. A cartoon; that is awarded, exhibited, shortlisted, submitted but not selected, or never submitted may therefore occupy different positions; sometimes in the centre and sometimes within the periphery; of an archive. These distinctions become significant when political cartoons are considered not only as forms of visual satire but also as records of the political and social circumstances from which they originally emerge.

The rationale for developing Margins Of Memory lies in the question of archival visibility. Political cartoons frequently respond to events that are temporally specific, yet their interpretation may depend upon linguistic, regional, political and media contexts that are not contained largely within the image itself. When such contextual information is separated from the cartoon, the historical circumstances of its production and circulation may become increasingly difficult to reconstruct. The archive therefore preserves each cartoon alongside information concerning its artist, date of publication, language, region, platform of circulation; mostly surrounding the provenance of the cartoon and its particular isolated political context. Lastly, it also involves the institutional selection history within the Indian Cartoon Gallery, where available.

The project adopts a participatory approach by allowing cartoonists to contribute their own works and the context is determined based on visual and provenance interpretation. This is significant because it allows the archive to retain forms of knowledge that may not be available through the image alone, particularly in relation to regional and vernacular political cartoons. The resulting collection is deliberately; a work in progress. The scale increases based on the number of submissions; thus currently, it functions as a participatory pilot archive rather than an comprehensive account of Indian Political Cartooning. Its purpose is to demonstrate how political cartoons can be situated within their contexts of production and circulation while remaining connected to existing institutional and digital archival structures.

Methodology

The methodology of Margins Of Memory is based on the collection, contextualization, description and digital organization of political cartoons. The primary materials for this were obtained through artist contributions, with contributors providing the cartoon image and accompanying information concerning its title, authorship, date, language, region, platform of circulation, provenance and political context. Where relevant, information concerning exhibition or competition history was also recorded. Original publication and social-media links were retained where they were available in order to maintain a connection between the archival record and the context in which the cartoon was originally circulated.

Selection, largely, was guided by the scope established in the counter-archival proposal. The collection focuses on Indian Political Cartoons that can be situated within identifiable political, regional, linguistic, or media contexts. Rather than treating institutional recognition as criterion of historical value, the archive records different forms of relationship with ICG, where such information is available. Thus, distinctions on the categorization of prized exhibits, unprized but shortlisted, submitted but not selected; are preserved as aspects of provenance and archival history.

The collection consists primarily of digitally supplied or digitally circulated images; whilst certain involve hand drawn cartoons but they aren’t dominating within the digital prospect. Each item is assigned a unique identifier beginning with MM, followed by a sequential number. The identifier establishes a consistent relationship with the image, its metadata, and its individual digital record. The archive’s metadata records include Archive ID, Title, Artist, Date, Language, Region, Platform, Provenance, ICG Selection History, Translation where applicable, Context. These fields were selected to preserve both bibliographic information and the contextual circumstances necessary for interpreting political cartoons as historical material.

The digital archive was constructed using HTML, CSS and JavaScript; while the platform used on it subsequently remained VS Code. A structural record system was used so that individual cartoons could be presented through a consistent archival interface while retaining their specific metadata and contextual descriptions. The platform also permits the archive to remain expandable without actually altering the structure of the existing records; considering the recently cataloged system. The website thus functions as a place where all the different aspects inclusive of origin, provenance, metadata, artwork and contextual information can be encountered together.

As per the Ethical considerations informed the collection process. Artist-contributed works are identified through provenance information, while source and publication details are retained where works originate from existing digital or print platforms. Contributors are asked to provide relevant permission for display of their work within the educational archive. It also involves a consent button that necessarily allows them to showcase transparency and engage in subsequent delivery of the illustrations. The project consequently distinguishes between the preservation and contextual presentation of a cartoon largely to accompany the archival record and ownership of the original artwork in place. The distinction is particularly important in a digital environment where accessibility and reproducibility do not necessarily constitute the same form of permission.
